> An RFC explores a problem **before** a decision exists. An ADR records the decision that results.
> If you already know the answer, write an ADR instead.

## Problem

What is wrong, who it affects, and how we know. Evidence, not assertion.

## Constraints

What must hold regardless of the solution — including principles P1–P8, existing ADRs, and
non-technical constraints such as operator capacity or procurement.

## Options

Explore genuinely. An RFC that presents one option with three strawmen has not done its job.

### Option A
### Option B
### Do nothing
**Always include this.** It is often the right answer, and forcing yourself to argue against it
surfaces whether the problem is real.

## Open questions

What would we need to know to decide? Can we find out cheaply?

## Recommendation

Optional. An RFC may legitimately conclude "we do not know enough yet, and here is the spike that
would tell us."
